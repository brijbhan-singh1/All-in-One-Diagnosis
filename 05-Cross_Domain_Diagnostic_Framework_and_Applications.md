The concept of diagnosis can be applied across almost every domain where we need to understand a problem, identify its root cause, assess the current state, and determine what action is required.

Traditionally, diagnosis is associated with healthcare, but the underlying process is much broader: observe symptoms → gather evidence → identify causes → assess severity → recommend corrective action.

## 3. What exactly can be diagnosed across these domains?

The deeper opportunity is that diagnosis can be applied not just to domains, but also to different types of problems.

A. Problems and failures

Something is not working as expected.

Examples: machine breakdown, software error, falling sales.

B. Root causes

The underlying factors responsible for an observed problem.

Examples: poor training, faulty components, inadequate demand forecasting.

C. Current state and performance

The condition of a system compared with a desired baseline.

Examples: organizational maturity, network health, student proficiency.

D. Risks and vulnerabilities

Conditions that could cause future problems.

Examples: financial exposure, security weaknesses, structural risks.

E. Deviations and anomalies

Unusual patterns or differences from expected behavior.

Examples: abnormal sensor readings, unusual transactions, unexpected test results.

F. Capability and improvement gaps

The difference between current performance and desired performance.

Examples: skills gaps, process inefficiencies, digital transformation readiness.

## 4. A universal diagnostic framework

A common framework can be adapted to almost any domain.

1. Observe

Collect symptoms, signals and evidence

2. Measure

Compare actual conditions with expected conditions

3. Diagnose

Identify possible causes and test hypotheses

4. Evaluate

Assess severity, impact, confidence and risk

5. Recommend action

Suggest corrective, preventive or improvement measures

For example, the same framework can diagnose a patient's symptoms, a failing machine, a company's declining revenue, or a student's learning difficulties. The evidence, terminology, expertise and acceptable level of certainty will differ by domain.

## 5. The bigger opportunity: cross-domain diagnosis

If you are thinking about building a universal diagnostic system, framework, AI agent or platform, there are several promising capabilities:

- Diagnostic reasoning: infer likely causes from observed evidence.
- Root-cause analysis: distinguish symptoms from underlying causes.
- Anomaly detection: identify what is unusual or unexpected.
- State assessment: determine the current condition or maturity of a system.
- Risk diagnosis: identify vulnerabilities and estimate potential impact.
- Predictive diagnosis: detect early warning signs before a failure occurs.
- Prescriptive diagnosis: recommend what to do next.
- Continuous diagnosis: monitor changes and update the assessment over time.
- Comparative diagnosis: compare entities, processes or systems against benchmarks.
- Explainable diagnosis: show the evidence and reasoning behind a conclusion.

The key distinction is that diagnosis identifies and explains a condition; prediction estimates what may happen; prescription recommends what to do. A comprehensive platform can combine all three.

One important limitation: not every domain has clearly defined symptoms, a single root cause, or a definitive diagnosis. In complex systems such as organizations, economies and ecosystems, the output may need to be a set of plausible explanations with confidence levels rather than one definitive answer.

To take this further, are you exploring diagnosis as a general concept, or are you trying to develop a cross-domain diagnostic framework or AI product? The second would allow us to map common diagnostic capabilities, reusable methods and domain-specific requirements systematically.
